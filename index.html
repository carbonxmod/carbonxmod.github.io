overview

Isomorphic keyboards are musical instruments with layouts in which any given sequence or combination of musical intervals, such as a scale or chord, forms the same shape, regardless of its root note.

This property provides a more intuitive mental model for understanding and learning the intricacies of music theory. Practicing scales and chords in every key becomes less repetitive, since transposing does not involve learning new finger movements.

Isomorphic keyboards ditch the legacy of decisions made over many centuries and offer us a clean slate: a medium where exploring new ideas is not constrained by history.

layout

Retrofitting a traditional keyboard with an isomorphic layout limits the alternative designs we can choose from. The least intrusive adaptation I could come up with involved only swapping out the original keys for custom ones.

The design borrows its layout from the Jankó keyboard, but reduces the number of rows to four to accommodate the limited vertical space available on the original instrument. The horizontal distance between keys remains the same as on a traditional keyboard, facilitating skill transfer.

The choice of hexagonal buttons is mostly aesthetic, but their larger surface area compared with circles also makes them easier to hit. The components were designed entirely in Fusion 360.

printing

The components were 3D printed on a Prusa i3 MK3 printer without any support material.

braces
The braces were printed upside down in PETG. The material’s flexibility helps the keys settle into place more easily, and its smoothness reduces friction when the keys are pressed.
keys
The keys were printed on their sides in PLA. The material’s stiffness keeps the components from bending when force is applied. I strongly recommend using PLA here; PETG makes the keys too flimsy to use.
buttons and sliders
The buttons were printed upright in PETG. Its silkier surface is more pleasant to touch. The buttons are the most exposed part of the design, and PETG also tends to resist ultraviolet light better. The sliders were also printed upright in PETG for similar reasons.

parts per octave

  • 1 brace
  • 6 long keys and 6 short keys
  • 14 white buttons and 10 black buttons

assembly

The modification was designed for the M-Audio Oxygen MK III MIDI controller, with any number of keys. To install the new keys, the original ones must be removed entirely.

  1. remove the original keys

    Open the keyboard by removing the screws on its underside. The original keys consist of three injection-molded parts per octave, which can be removed by undoing their screws.

  2. assemble each octave

    Assemble the components into sections spanning one octave. Insert the keys into their slots in the brace and hold them in place with steel wire 1.1 mm in diameter. The buttons should snap onto the keys when pressed.

  3. fit the new keybed

    When placing the assembled sections in the keyboard, make sure the hooks on the bottom of the keys sit under the keyboard’s dampener strip.

  4. fasten and close

    Fasten the sections with screws, but use shorter ones or add printed spacers above all the screw holes in the braces. Otherwise, the screws will poke holes in the bottom of the keyboard, since the original keys had a higher base than the braces. Screw the bottom of the keyboard back on.

experience

Engineering my way out of tedious piano practice did not play out as I had planned: learning the new layout takes serious effort. While the dexterity acquired through piano practice certainly helps a lot, old muscle memories unfortunately do not transfer as well.

I have always played piano just for fun, without any serious ambitions, so I do not feel the urge to go back to the original layout. I prefer exploring the patterns unfolding on the new layout to returning to the comfort of my fading piano skills.

All that said, if you decide to build this device and approach it as a new, unfamiliar instrument, you will probably find some joyful moments playing it.
